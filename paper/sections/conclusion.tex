\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We asked how far vision--language models can be shrunk, quantized and starved of pixels before they fail to detect a person who is down, and what each concession saves on local hardware. On \BenchNKept{} visually reviewed frames from two public datasets, small open VLMs answered zero-shot with F1 up to \RBestFone, far above a pose-and-rule baseline whose detector often fails to see a person on the floor. Most of the efficiency gain stems from three choices that cost little accuracy: 4-bit weights, which halve memory without altering GPU latency; input reduced to 512\,px, or for LFM2.5-VL-1.6B to 256\,px; and a 1.6\,B model in place of a 3--4\,B one. Two seemingly prudent choices proved detrimental: detailed prompts that enumerate the postures to exclude, and short multi-frame clips. The largest single improvement for every model came from replacing its yes/no answer with a threshold on its yes-probability, set on labelled data.

On our device, the integrated GPU sustains several cameras at one decision per second with a 1.6\,B model at about \PLfmMQfourTwofiftysixOneGpuJ\,J per decision, whereas the CPU alone sustains one camera with the 0.45\,B model and expends \PCpuGpuRatioMin--\PCpuGpuRatioMax{} times more energy per decision. Future work should replicate these measurements on embedded boards, test on industrial footage, aggregate decisions over time, and ask whether light fine-tuning or detector-guided prompting closes the residual gap on kneeling and crouching people.
